Fetal Echocardiography: When Your Baby Needs a Detailed Heart Scan During Pregnancy

A fetal echocardiogram takes a closer look at your baby's developing heart. Learn when it may be advised, what happens during the scan and what results can mean.

Quick answer

Fetal echocardiography is a specialised ultrasound that examines the baby's heart, including chambers, valves, blood vessels, rhythm and flow. It may be recommended if a scan raises concern or family or maternal factors increase the chance of congenital heart disease.

Hearing the words “fetal echo” after an ultrasound can leave parents with a lot of questions. Why is another scan needed? Did the earlier scan show something abnormal? Is there a problem with the baby's heart?

A fetal echocardiogram is a detailed ultrasound examination of the baby's heart before birth. It allows a specialist to examine the heart more closely than the cardiac views obtained during a routine pregnancy ultrasound.

Being advised to have one does not, by itself, mean that a heart problem has been found. Sometimes the reason is a finding on an earlier scan. In other pregnancies, a maternal condition, family history or another risk factor may make a more detailed assessment appropriate.

Here is what fetal echocardiography involves, when doctors may recommend it, what it can show and what parents can expect from the result.

What is fetal echocardiography?

Fetal echocardiography is essentially a closer look at the baby's heart before birth.

During a routine pregnancy scan, the sonologist assesses the baby's overall anatomy. The heart is an important part of that examination. A fetal echocardiogram goes further by using a more detailed set of cardiac views.

The specialist looks at how the different parts of the heart are formed and connected. Depending on the clinical situation, the assessment can include:

  • The four chambers of the heart
  • The heart valves
  • The outflow tracts
  • The major blood vessels
  • Heart rhythm
  • Cardiac movement
  • Blood flow through selected structures

This matters because not all heart abnormalities are obvious from a single view.

For parents, the simplest distinction is this:

An anomaly scan looks at the baby's whole body, including the heart. A fetal echocardiogram concentrates specifically on the heart.

ISUOG recommends a systematic fetal cardiac examination that includes assessment beyond the four-chamber view, because some congenital heart abnormalities may not be apparent from the four-chamber view alone.

When is a fetal echo done?

A detailed fetal echocardiogram is commonly performed around 18 to 22 weeks of pregnancy.

That timing is not an absolute rule. The appropriate timing depends on why the examination has been recommended, what was seen on an earlier scan and the individual pregnancy.

In selected situations, cardiac assessment may be considered earlier. Because the fetal heart is very small at an early gestational age, an early examination may sometimes need to be followed by another assessment later in pregnancy.

So if your doctor recommends a fetal echo at a different stage of pregnancy, it does not necessarily mean that something is wrong. The timing is determined by the clinical reason for the examination.

Why would a doctor recommend a fetal echocardiogram?

There isn't one single reason for a fetal echo.

Sometimes the scan is recommended because the doctor has actually seen something that needs a closer look. In other cases, the baby's heart looks normal on screening, but the pregnancy has a factor associated with a higher risk of congenital heart disease.

Some of the situations in which fetal echocardiography may be considered include the following.

A possible heart problem on the ultrasound

This is an important reason for referral.

During the routine anomaly scan, the doctor may notice that one of the cardiac views is unusual or difficult to interpret. There may be a question about the four chambers, the heart's position, the outflow tracts, the major vessels or the rhythm.

That does not automatically mean that the baby has a heart defect.

Sometimes the fetal position makes the images difficult to obtain. Sometimes an apparent finding needs a more detailed examination before its significance can be understood.

A fetal echocardiogram can help answer that question.

Increased nuchal translucency

Nuchal translucency, or NT, is a measurement obtained during the first-trimester ultrasound.

An increased NT measurement can be associated with several fetal conditions, including congenital heart disease. It is a risk marker, however, rather than a diagnosis.

The decision to perform fetal echocardiography depends on the NT measurement and the rest of the clinical picture.

The 2023 American Society of Echocardiography recommendations identify an NT measurement of 3.5 mm or greater as an indication for fetal echocardiography. For measurements between 3.0 and 3.4 mm, fetal echocardiography may be considered depending on the clinical setting and other factors.

So an increased NT does not mean that a heart abnormality has been confirmed. It means that additional assessment may be appropriate.

A previous baby or pregnancy had congenital heart disease

If a previous child or pregnancy was affected by congenital heart disease, your doctor may recommend a fetal echocardiogram in a subsequent pregnancy.

The significance of the history depends on the type of heart condition and whether an underlying genetic cause is known.

Having a previous affected pregnancy does not mean that the same condition will occur again. It is one factor that may influence the decision to perform a more detailed fetal cardiac examination.

Diabetes that existed before pregnancy

Pre-existing, or pre-gestational, diabetes is another recognised indication for fetal echocardiography.

This is worth distinguishing from gestational diabetes. The two situations are not automatically treated as equivalent when deciding whether a fetal echo is required.

The 2023 ASE recommendations list pre-gestational diabetes as an indication for fetal echocardiography. Gestational diabetes diagnosed later in pregnancy, in the setting of a normal cardiac screening examination, does not carry the same recommendation.

Your doctor will consider the type of diabetes, when it was diagnosed and the findings from the pregnancy scans.

Certain genetic or fetal findings

Some genetic conditions and fetal ultrasound findings are associated with congenital heart abnormalities.

If such a finding is identified, the doctor may recommend a fetal echocardiogram along with other appropriate investigations or genetic counselling.

The reason for the recommendation depends on the specific finding. There is no single approach that applies to every genetic screening result or ultrasound marker.

Certain maternal conditions

Some maternal medical conditions can increase the risk of fetal cardiac problems.

For example, certain autoimmune conditions involving SSA/SSB antibodies can be relevant to fetal cardiac assessment. Other specific maternal conditions may also lead to referral.

This is why the doctor's recommendation is based on the complete medical history rather than on one isolated factor.

IVF Pregnancy

Pregnancy following IVF may be another reason for discussing fetal echocardiography.

However, IVF alone does not mean that every pregnancy requires a fetal echo.

The 2023 ASE recommendations describe IVF as a situation in which fetal echocardiography may be considered, depending partly on the quality of the routine cardiac screening and the circumstances of the pregnancy.

Your obstetrician or fetal medicine specialist can explain whether an additional heart scan is appropriate in your particular case.

What does a fetal echocardiogram check?

A fetal echo involves more than simply looking at whether the baby's heart is beating.

The specialist assesses the anatomy and function of the developing cardiovascular system using multiple ultrasound views.

The four chambers

The fetal heart has four chambers: two atria and two ventricles.

The specialist examines their size, structure, relationship and movement.

Differences between the chambers can sometimes provide clues that further assessment is needed.

The heart's position and axis

The position of the heart within the baby's chest is also assessed.

The cardiac axis, which describes the orientation of the heart, can provide useful information when evaluating fetal cardiac anatomy.

The heart valves

The valves between the heart chambers and the major vessels are assessed as part of the detailed examination.

Their movement and associated blood flow can help the specialist evaluate how the heart is functioning.

The outflow tracts

The specialist examines the pathways through which blood leaves the heart.

This is particularly important because some congenital heart abnormalities may not be obvious when looking only at the four chambers.

The major blood vessels

The relationship and appearance of the major vessels connected to the heart are assessed using appropriate ultrasound views.

These views can provide information that is not available from the basic four-chamber examination alone.

Heart rhythm

The specialist also assesses the fetal heart rhythm.

If an irregular rhythm is suspected, further monitoring or assessment may be advised depending on the circumstances.

Blood flow

Doppler ultrasound can be used to examine blood flow through selected parts of the fetal circulation.

This provides additional information about the cardiovascular system beyond simply looking at the heart's structure.

Fetal echo vs anomaly scan: what's the difference?

This is one of the most common questions parents have.

The two examinations overlap, but they are not the same.

Think of the anomaly scan as a broad examination of the baby, while fetal echocardiography is a specialised examination of one particular organ system.

Neither scan completely replaces the other.

Can an anomaly scan detect heart problems?

Yes. Routine fetal cardiac screening can identify many important heart abnormalities.

But it cannot identify every possible congenital heart condition.

The quality of the examination can be affected by factors such as:

  • The baby's position
  • Gestational age
  • Maternal body habitus
  • Amniotic fluid
  • Image quality
  • Equipment
  • The experience of the examiner
  • The particular heart condition being assessed

Some abnormalities are also difficult to see before birth.

This is why a normal anomaly scan is reassuring but should not be interpreted as a guarantee that every possible heart condition has been excluded.

If the screening examination raises a concern, a detailed fetal echo may provide the additional information needed.

What happens during a fetal echocardiogram?

For most patients, the examination is performed through the abdomen using an ultrasound probe.

You will generally lie in a comfortable position while the specialist examines the baby using ultrasound. Gel is placed on the abdomen to allow the probe to produce clear images.

The examination may take longer than a standard ultrasound because the specialist needs to obtain several different views of the fetal heart.

You may see images of the heart on the monitor while the examination is being performed. The specialist may use colour Doppler or other ultrasound techniques to assess blood flow.

The baby does not always stay in a convenient position.

If the fetal back is facing the wrong direction, for example, some cardiac views may be difficult to obtain. The specialist may try again after the baby moves or may recommend a repeat examination.

An examination that is limited by fetal position is not the same as an examination showing an abnormal heart.

Is fetal echocardiography safe?

Fetal echocardiography uses ultrasound.

It does not use X-rays and does not involve inserting a needle into the uterus.

Ultrasound is widely used during pregnancy when medically indicated. Fetal echocardiography should be performed by appropriately trained professionals using suitable equipment and examination protocols.

If you have been advised to undergo the examination, your doctor can explain why it has been recommended and what information they hope to obtain.

What if the fetal echo finds something abnormal?

This is often the part parents worry about most.

First, an abnormal fetal echo does not tell you the whole story by itself. Congenital heart disease includes many different conditions, and they can vary considerably in severity.

The specialist will usually explain:

  • What part of the heart appears different
  • Whether the finding is definite or still uncertain
  • How the finding may affect heart function
  • Whether other abnormalities are present
  • Whether another scan is needed
  • Whether genetic counselling or additional testing should be considered
  • Whether a paediatric cardiologist or another specialist should be involved

In some pregnancies, the information may also help the medical team plan delivery and newborn care.

The goal of finding a heart abnormality during pregnancy is to give the parents and medical team time to understand the condition and plan appropriate care.

Does an abnormal fetal echo mean the baby has a severe heart problem?

No.

There are many different types of congenital heart abnormalities.

Some are relatively mild. Others require specialist treatment after birth. Some findings identified during pregnancy may also require additional imaging before their significance becomes clear.

For this reason, the wording of an ultrasound report should not be interpreted in isolation.

If your report mentions a possible cardiac abnormality, discuss it with the fetal medicine specialist or paediatric cardiology team involved in your care.

A suspected finding is not the same thing as a confirmed diagnosis.

Can fetal echocardiography detect every heart defect?

No.

This is an important limitation of even a detailed fetal heart examination.

Prenatal ultrasound can provide a great deal of information about the developing heart, but it cannot reproduce the same assessment that can be made after birth.

Some heart defects are difficult to detect during pregnancy. Others may become more apparent as the pregnancy progresses or only after the baby's circulation changes following birth.

Image quality also matters.

A baby's position, gestational age, maternal body habitus, the amount of amniotic fluid and the specific condition being looked for can all influence what can be seen.

So the right way to understand a normal fetal echocardiogram is that the baby's heart appeared normal within the limits of the examination. It is not a guarantee that no cardiac condition will ever be diagnosed.

What if the baby is in the wrong position?

This happens quite often during pregnancy ultrasound.

The baby's position can make it difficult to obtain a particular cardiac view. Sometimes the specialist simply needs to wait for the baby to move.

Depending on the situation, the examiner may:

  • Change the mother's position
  • Try different ultrasound angles
  • Wait for fetal movement
  • Reassess the heart later
  • Arrange a repeat scan if necessary

If you are asked to return because the examination was incomplete, that does not automatically mean an abnormality was found.

It may simply mean that the specialist could not obtain all the images needed to complete the assessment.

Does every pregnancy need a fetal echo?

No.

A separate fetal echocardiogram is not automatically required in every pregnancy.

The fetal heart is routinely assessed during the appropriate pregnancy ultrasound. A dedicated fetal echo is generally considered when there is a specific indication.

These may include:

  • A suspected heart abnormality on an ultrasound
  • Certain increased NT measurements
  • A previous child or pregnancy affected by congenital heart disease
  • Pre-existing maternal diabetes
  • Certain maternal autoimmune conditions
  • Selected genetic or fetal findings
  • Some IVF pregnancies
  • Other clinical circumstances identified by the treating specialist

The exact indications can vary depending on professional guidance, local screening practices and the details of the pregnancy.

Does a high NT mean my baby needs a fetal echo?

Not every increased NT measurement means the baby has a heart problem.

NT is a screening marker. An increased measurement can be associated with chromosomal conditions, structural abnormalities and congenital heart disease, but it does not diagnose any of them.

The measurement itself is one factor doctors consider when deciding whether fetal echocardiography is appropriate.

For example, the 2023 ASE recommendations consider NT ≥3.5 mm an indication for fetal echocardiography, while measurements of 3.0 to 3.4 mm may warrant consideration depending on the circumstances.

Your doctor may also take into account other ultrasound findings, screening results and your pregnancy history.

Does diabetes mean a fetal echo is necessary?

It depends on the type and timing of diabetes.

Pre-gestational diabetes is recognised as a reason to consider fetal echocardiography.

Gestational diabetes diagnosed later in pregnancy is different. According to the 2023 ASE recommendations, gestational diabetes diagnosed after the second trimester, when the fetal cardiac screening examination is normal, is not by itself an indication for fetal echocardiography.

This distinction is easy to miss when reading general information online.

If you have diabetes during pregnancy, your obstetrician can tell you whether your individual history warrants a fetal cardiac assessment.

What questions should you ask before or after a fetal echo?

It is completely reasonable to ask questions before the examination.

You may want to ask:

  1. Why has a fetal echo been recommended for my pregnancy?
  2. Was there anything unusual on the previous scan?
  3. Is this being advised because of my medical history or family history?
  4. What exactly will the specialist look at?
  5. Was the examination completed successfully?
  6. Does the baby's heart appear normal?
  7. If something was seen, is it confirmed or does it need another scan?
  8. Will I need another fetal echo later?
  9. Should I meet a paediatric cardiologist or genetic counsellor?
  10. Could the finding affect delivery or care after birth?

These questions can be especially useful if you have been referred because of an unexpected ultrasound finding.

When should you see a fetal medicine specialist?

A fetal medicine consultation may be appropriate when a pregnancy requires more detailed assessment than routine obstetric care can provide.

You may be referred if:

  • A scan shows a possible fetal abnormality
  • There is an increased NT measurement
  • A fetal heart problem is suspected
  • A previous pregnancy or child had congenital heart disease
  • You have a medical condition associated with increased fetal risk
  • A genetic screening result requires further assessment
  • You are carrying twins or multiples and need specialised monitoring
  • You want a second opinion about a fetal scan

A fetal medicine specialist can review the ultrasound findings together with the pregnancy history and explain what further assessment, if any, would be useful.

Fetal echo and fetal neurosonography: are they the same?

No.

Both are specialised fetal ultrasound examinations, but they examine different parts of the baby's anatomy.

Fetal echocardiography focuses on the baby's heart and cardiovascular system.

Fetal neurosonography provides a more detailed assessment of the fetal brain and central nervous system.

A doctor may recommend either examination, or both, depending on the findings and risk factors in a particular pregnancy.

Can a fetal echo be done with the anomaly scan?

The examinations are often performed during a similar period of pregnancy, but they are not simply two names for the same scan.

The anomaly scan provides an overall assessment of fetal anatomy, including standard cardiac screening.

A fetal echocardiogram is a more focused examination of the heart and is usually recommended when there is a specific reason for detailed cardiac assessment.

Whether the examinations are scheduled together or separately depends on the clinical circumstances and the imaging service available.

What happens if the fetal echo is normal?

If the specialist finds that the baby's heart appears normal, they will explain the result and whether any further cardiac follow-up is necessary.

For many pregnancies, no additional specialised cardiac assessment is required unless another finding or risk factor changes the plan.

It is still useful to remember that fetal echocardiography has limitations. A normal result is reassuring, but it cannot exclude every heart condition that might become apparent later in pregnancy or after birth.

Being sent for a fetal echocardiogram can sound worrying, particularly if nobody has explained why the scan is being recommended.

In many cases, the reason is simply that the doctor wants a more detailed look at the baby's heart. Sometimes there is a finding on the anomaly scan that needs clarification. At other times, the recommendation is based on a known risk factor rather than something abnormal seen in the baby.

A fetal echo can provide valuable information about the developing heart, but it also has limits. A normal result is reassuring, while an unusual finding needs to be interpreted in context rather than assumed to represent a severe condition.

If you have been advised to undergo fetal echocardiography, ask what prompted the referral, what the specialist will be looking for and whether any follow-up is likely to be needed. Those answers can make the purpose of the examination much clearer.

Key takeaways

  1. Fetal echocardiography is a specialised ultrasound examination of the baby's developing heart.
  2. It is more detailed than the cardiac assessment included in a routine anomaly scan.
  3. A suspected cardiac finding on ultrasound is an important reason for performing a fetal echo.
  4. Certain maternal, fetal and family-history factors can also lead to referral.
  5. A detailed fetal echo is commonly performed around 18 to 22 weeks, although the timing can vary.
  6. Increased NT is a risk marker, not a diagnosis of congenital heart disease.
  7. Pre-existing maternal diabetes is one recognised indication for fetal echocardiography.
  8. IVF pregnancy alone does not automatically mean that a fetal echo is required.
  9. A normal fetal echo is reassuring but cannot rule out every possible heart condition.
  10. An abnormal result does not automatically mean that the baby has a severe heart problem.
  11. Further assessment may include repeat imaging, genetic counselling or consultation with paediatric cardiac specialists, depending on the finding.
  12. The best interpretation of a fetal echo comes from considering the scan together with the mother's medical history, previous investigations and the overall pregnancy.
